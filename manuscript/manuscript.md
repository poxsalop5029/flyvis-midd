# Emergence and variability of static-pattern decoded motion in a connectome-constrained visual network

## Abstract

Static motion illusion-like discrete design (MIDD) patterns are physically stationary, yet can evoke directional motion readouts. We examined these responses in connectome-constrained FlyVis networks trained for optic-flow estimation without neural-response supervision. The antisymmetric component was near zero at initialization ($|A|<3\times10^{-9}$) and emerged during ordinary optic-flow learning, although MIDD was not a training target. For 3MIDD C=48 across phases in the official 50-model cohort, absolute-agreement ICC(A,1) was 0.7647, and 34/50 models changed the sign of $A$. For a centered eight-field rendering, measured local three-level responses conditionally predicted held-out four-level scalar outputs (mean $R^2=0.9024$); this affine prediction used measured local responses at held-out luminances and does not imply vector-field additivity. Sector shuffles and other static controls produced comparable or larger responses; canonical 72/96-sector patterns were not normalized-response maxima in any of ten models, and the decoded output was not selective for MIDD under these tested conditions. Three model-level associations with Sintel endpoint error did not survive Holm correction. Decoder-input replacements altered both MIDD and physical-motion readouts. Network–decoder cross-swaps changed decoded magnitude and direction readout, but only 9/90 mismatched pairs correctly decoded both physical directions. Decoded flow is a model output, not perception.

## Introduction

Stationary luminance patterns can elicit motion-related behavior in Drosophila, as well as apparent motion in human observers. @AgrochaoEtAl2020IllusoryMotionFlies reported analogous behavioral responses to static motion-illusion patterns in flies and humans and implicated direction-selective T4/T5 neurons in the fly response. This behavioral result makes the fly visual system a relevant setting for asking how motion signals can arise from spatial structure without physical image motion. This motivates a shared question about visual computation, not a claim of identical percepts or mechanisms across species.

The Drosophila pathway links computation to identified circuitry: T4/T5 provide direction-selective ON/OFF signals, and columnar cell types and synapses are mapped [@MaisakEtAl2013DirectionMap; @StrotherEtAl2017DirectionalSelectivity]. Flies and humans can share motion-estimation strategies [@ClarkEtAl2014MotionStrategy], while ON/OFF asymmetries relate circuit responses to natural-scene velocity estimates [@FitzgeraldEtAl2011StimulusStatistics; @LeonhardtEtAl2016ONOFF]. This makes the fly pathway a tractable setting for studying static-pattern responses.

FlyVis provides a model in which this question can be asked with the visual connectome held fixed. The recurrent network maps onto identified fly visual cell types and is trained end-to-end for optic-flow estimation from rendered Sintel videos, following the original FlyVis task rather than introducing a new training objective here [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. Sintel is a computer-generated movie dataset, not a sample of a fly's natural visual experience. The task supplies a computational objective for a motion pathway; it does not make the model's output a biological behavior. Training does not use neural-response supervision, yet the ensemble median correctly predicted measured ON/OFF preferences for all 32 cell types with established preferences; the best-performing individual model matched 30/32. The networks also recovered ON-motion preference in T4, OFF-motion preference in T5, and the lack of direction selectivity reported for nine T4/T5 input types (Mi1, Tm3, Mi4, Mi9, Tm1, Tm2, Tm4, Tm9, and CT1). Predictions were not uniformly correct, including a reversed Tm4 response [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. FlyVis is therefore an empirically compared, constrained model, not a complete account of fly vision.

In the fly, local T4/T5 motion signals feed downstream wide-field circuits, including lobula-plate tangential cells such as HS and VS, which integrate optic-flow patterns for self-motion and optomotor responses [@BorstEgelhaaf1989MotionDetection; @LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. FlyVis replaces this downstream biological readout with a learned two-layer convolutional decoder that maps recurrent activity to a dense optical-flow field. The decoder receives instantaneous activity, while temporal integration and motion-sensitive dynamics arise in the network; it is a readout of network computation, not a stand-alone motion detector. The readout can shape measured output. Decoded flow is therefore a model-level measure for comparing static-pattern responses across network states, learned solutions, and readout pairings, not fly perception or behavior.

A static-pattern response would connect fly behavior for static patterns with task learning in a connectome-constrained motion network. It also raises how independently optimized models with the same wiring can arrive at different solutions [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. MIDD varies local luminance sequences and their spatial combination. Human psychophysics related responses to four-level patterns to component three-level patterns [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD]; we test a related scalar composition without equating human and model measurements. Static-illusion output and variability across trained instances have also been reported in a video-prediction network [@WatanabeEtAl2018IllusoryMotion; @KirubeswaranStorrs2023InconsistentMotion].

We ask whether static MIDD evokes directional output in optic-flow-trained FlyVis, how that output develops during learning, and what computations could produce it. We distinguish the Original/Mirror-dependent component from output shared across the pair, test local-to-global scalar composition, and compare FlyVis with a classical nonlinear motion-detector array as a computational reference. We also ask why the same output-level relations have different expressions across models, phases, and renderings, including how the trained network–decoder pair shapes the readout. This design treats decoded flow as a model output and does not equate it with perception or behavior.

## Results

### Static patterns evoke decoded motion across FlyVis models

We asked whether static MIDD patterns produce directional output in optic-flow-trained networks. Repeated 3MIDD and 4MIDD Original/Mirror frames generated organized decoded vector fields across 50 official models (Fig. 1). Raw reversal was defined at phase zero by opposite signs of Original and Mirror mean signed tangential flow ($T_O T_M<0$); 21/50 models reversed at 3MIDD C=48. Measurable output and reversal are distinct outcomes.

Phase zero is the shared reference for Figs. 1, 2, and 5; Fig. 4 evaluates its phase dependence. Balanced negative patterns also produced measurable output. Physical clockwise and counterclockwise propeller controls gave the expected decoded tangential signs in 50/50 models (Supplementary Fig. S1). In three selected models, decoded output remained measurable in the 9–10 s window of prolonged static presentation (Supplementary Fig. S3).

**Output-level decomposition.** We define $A=(T_O-T_M)/2$ and $S=(T_O+T_M)/2$ for the Original/Mirror tangential outputs; the raw pair reverses exactly when $|A|>|S|$ (full identities in Methods; Fig. 4). This is an exact relation among decoded scalar outputs.
### Static decoded motion is not specific to canonical MIDD

We evaluated 15 intermediate luminances in the native BoxEye rendering across the official 50 models (Fig. 2). The ensemble-mean antisymmetric response $A$ was negative from C=16–112 and positive from C=128–239. At the phase-zero reference, mean A was −0.00935 for C=48 and +0.00826 for C=207, with raw reversal in 21/50 and 18/50 models, respectively. For 4MIDD C1=85/C2=170, mean A was +0.00321 and reversal occurred in 12/50 models. Negative controls had nonzero mean tangential output in all three conditions.

Histogram-preserving sector shuffles produced antisymmetric magnitudes comparable to or larger than MIDD: canonical versus shuffled means were 0.01062/0.01331 (C=48), 0.00940/0.01260 (C=207), and 0.00340/0.01170 (4MIDD; Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S5; Table S2). Uniform gray, histogram-matched random pixels, and balanced negatives also produced measurable output; these tests show no greater response to MIDD than to the tested static controls. In a separate ten-model sweep, canonical 72/96-sector stimuli were not the normalized-response maximum in any model; response varied with spatial frequency, edge density, and sampling (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Static output was also broader than MIDD: across 14 learning seeds, sector-shuffle, balanced-negative, and uniform-input responses changed during optic-flow training (Supplementary Fig. S8). Thus, decoded motion occurred for multiple structured and unstructured inputs, without evidence of MIDD specificity.

### Local three-level responses predict global four-level antisymmetric scalar motion

We tested whether the global four-level antisymmetric scalar follows an affine composition law based on independently measured local three-level components. In ten official models and a common centered eight-field rendering, the relationship was

$$
A_4(C_1,C_2)\approx \alpha\,[A_3(C_1)-A_3(C_2)]+\beta.
$$

Across seven luminance levels, model-wise fits had mean $R^2=0.9295$ and the unfitted component signs agreed with observed signs for 96.5% of evaluable pairs (Fig. 3). The mean fitted slope was 0.8292 and the intercept was 0.00737, so this is an affine composition law rather than identity or exact linear summation.

Conditional leave-one-luminance-out mean held-out $R^2$ was 0.9024 (Supplementary Fig. S7; Table S6). Because the A3 value at the held luminance remained measured, this is conditional prediction from local component responses, not prediction without those measurements. Physical CW/CCW calibration gave negative scalar output for CW and positive output for CCW in all ten models. The result is specific to the centered eight-field rendering and scalar projection; it does not establish full-vector-field additivity. It resembles the compositional form reported in human MIDD psychophysics, but the model and human responses were not directly compared [@KobayashiWatanabe2023MIDD].

The strength of this scalar relationship depended on rendering. In a separate seven-field comparison, fitted mean $R^2$ was 0.439 with BoxEye and 0.934 with highres_232, with sign agreement of 0.458 and 0.975, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2). The strong affine composition is therefore established for the centered eight-field condition; transfer to other rendering geometries is limited. This scalar result does not establish additivity of the full vector field.

We compared the FlyVis output with a classical Reichardt-detector array as a computational reference (Supplementary Fig. S4). Summing the local detector outputs linearly yielded near-zero directional output for static 3MIDD and 4MIDD. Applying the sign-preserving saturation used in the Bach-type array to each local detector before spatial summation instead produced nonzero static directional residuals [@BachAtalaGerard2020RotatingSnakes]. After calibrating the model's sign against physically rotating stimuli, the array and FlyVis had the same net tangential direction and the same response-magnitude ordering (3MIDD greater than 4MIDD) for the canonical patterns.

The calibrated sign comparison concerns raw tangential output, not the mirror-antisymmetric component $A$. The agreement was partial. In a separate 21×21 4MIDD luminance landscape for FlyVis model 000, the mirror-antisymmetric directional components showed 81.0% descriptive sign agreement over robust grid points and Pearson correlation $r=0.455$. These grid values are a descriptive census, not independent trials or an inferential test. The FlyVis mirror-symmetric component had 3.14 times the RMS of its directional component. Near the canonical 4MIDD setting, the directional component itself had opposite signs in the two models; the larger positive FlyVis shared component determined the positive raw output. Thus the calibrated raw-output agreement does not imply that the models share the full directional component. The classical array demonstrates that standard nonlinear motion-detector computation can generate part of the directional structure, but it does not explain the full FlyVis response or identify the nonlinearity that generates it in FlyVis. Follow-up seed and mechanism analyses also did not establish a single upstream nonlinear residual as a universal explanation.

### Response sign and magnitude vary across models and stimulus phase

We quantified phase dependence for C=48 across the 50 official models at twelve phases of the 15° color-unit period (Fig. 4). Absolute-agreement ICC(A,1) across phases was 0.7647, while 34/50 models changed the sign of A across phases. Thus, phase consistency at the ensemble level coexists with frequent within-model sign changes. At phase zero, mean A was −0.00935; averaging across phases gave −0.00378. The phase-zero and phase-averaged model signs agreed in 39/50 models (Table S6).

Thus, models with the same connectome differ in decoded-output sign and magnitude, which can also change with stimulus alignment. Phase-zero values in Figs. 1, 2, and 5 are reference-condition results. Additional placement and receptor-sampling analyses also changed the measured A and S components, supporting a role for rendering geometry in the measured response (Supplementary Figs. S5, S6).

### Static-pattern responses emerge during optic-flow learning

We followed 14 independently trained learning-custom seeds from initialization through common training checkpoints (Fig. 5). At initialization, $|A|$ was below $3\times10^{-9}$ for every seed and stimulus family. During ordinary optic-flow training, antisymmetric magnitude increased; at step 100,000, the median $|A|$ normalized by paired mean flow magnitude was 0.03832 for C=48, 0.03493 for C=207, and 0.02449 for 4MIDD. MIDD was evaluated after training and was not a training target.

At 100,000 steps, $A$ was negative in 12/14 seeds for C=48, positive in 13/14 for C=207, and positive in 11/14 for 4MIDD. The two-sided exact binomial $p$ values were 0.0129, 0.00183, and 0.0574; Bonferroni-adjusted values across these three conditions were 0.0388, 0.0055, and 0.1721, respectively (Table S6). The majority direction was maintained at every checkpoint from 90,000 through 100,000 steps: 11–12/14 seeds were negative for C=48, 13/14 were positive for C=207, and 11–12/14 were positive for 4MIDD. Thus, the sign tendency was robust over this late checkpoint window for C=48 and C=207, but not statistically supported for 4MIDD after correction. Other static controls also developed measurable responses during the same training interval (Supplementary Fig. S8). The effect on the raw Original/Mirror pair depended on the joint decoded values of $A$ and $S$; these seed-level results describe output variation and do not identify differences in internal implementation. Sintel EPE improved during training, but task performance alone did not uniquely specify this static-pattern phenotype. Responses to other controls likewise show that this emergence was not specific to MIDD.


Across the official 50 models, Sintel endpoint error had a Spearman association of $\rho=0.284$ with signed $A$ (95% bootstrap CI [−0.003, 0.534], raw $p=0.0458$, Holm-adjusted $p=0.0915$), $\rho=−0.215$ with $|A|$ (95% CI [−0.502, 0.096], $p=0.134$, adjusted $p=0.134$), and $\rho=−0.326$ with phase variability, measured as $\mathrm{SD}(A)$ across phases divided by mean paired flow magnitude (95% CI [−0.586, −0.023], $p=0.0208$, adjusted $p=0.0624$). None of the three associations remained below 0.05 after Holm correction; these data do not establish a reliable relation between task error and the decoded MIDD phenotype (Table S2).

### Broad interventions and network–decoder pairing constrain decoded output

We compared decoder-input replacement across static MIDD and physical-motion stimuli in official models 000–009 (Fig. 6B–C; Supplementary Fig. S9). Targeted channels were replaced by frame-matched uniform-gray activity while recurrent and non-target activity remained intact. The primary endpoint was the signed intervention-minus-no-op contrast normalized by the paired no-op mean flow magnitude. For C=48 MIDD, mean normalized changes were +0.05449 for T4 replacement (95% model-bootstrap CI [0.01361, 0.10001]) and +0.06696 for T4+T5 replacement (95% CI [0.03052, 0.11040]). The same endpoint showed changes for both propeller and moving-edge contrasts in the same cohort. Because the operation affected physical-motion as well as static-pattern output, these results are consistent with a contribution from general motion-processing/readout machinery rather than a MIDD-selective operation; they do not identify a cell-specific causal mechanism.

In a separate 10×10 cross-swap, each of ten selected official networks (000, 005, 010, 015, 020, 025, 030, 035, 040, 045) was paired with each selected decoder. Over stable frames 10–49, mean matched $|A|$ was 0.00902, compared with 0.02796 for the within-network mean across nine mismatched decoders (Fig. 6D; Supplementary Fig. S10). Both rightward and leftward physical directions were decoded correctly for 10/10 matched pairs but only 9/90 mismatched pairs (Fig. 6E; Supplementary Fig. S10; Tables S3 and S6). The 90 mismatched combinations are structured pairings, not independent models, and their values are summarized within network. Because mismatched pairs also generally failed ordinary physical-motion readout, the larger mismatched $|A|$ cannot be interpreted as a MIDD-specific pairing effect. The results show that decoded magnitude and physical-direction readout depend on network–decoder pairing, consistent with a co-adapted readout, but they do not causally decompose network, decoder, and interaction contributions.

## Discussion

Static MIDD patterns evoked directional decoded flow in optic-flow-trained FlyVis networks, but the response was not specific to canonical MIDD: sector shuffles and other static controls produced comparable or larger outputs. The findings therefore concern how task-trained motion networks and their readouts respond to static structure, not a dedicated illusion response.

This output-level decomposition summarizes the measured pair without identifying separate neural pathways. Under the centered eight-field rendering, measured local three-level responses conditionally predicted held-out four-level scalar outputs (mean $R^2=0.9024$). This affine prediction has a non-unit slope and depends on rendering; the separate seven-field BoxEye comparison was weaker. The result is a conditional scalar prediction, not a full-vector identity.

### Candidate computational account

One candidate account is that nonlinear processing leaves local directional residuals that do not cancel during spatial aggregation. The classical Reichardt array produced such static residuals, and its calibrated raw tangential direction agreed with FlyVis for the canonical 3MIDD and 4MIDD conditions. The correspondence was partial: near canonical 4MIDD, the mirror-antisymmetric components had opposite signs, and a one-model luminance landscape showed descriptive rather than inferential agreement. This comparison establishes a computational possibility, not the causal mechanism in FlyVis.

### Learning and output variability

The antisymmetric component emerged from near zero during ordinary optic-flow learning, although MIDD was not a training target. Across official models, absolute-agreement ICC(A,1) across phases was 0.7647, while 34/50 changed the sign of $A$ with phase. In learning seeds, the majority sign tendencies at C=48 and C=207 persisted across the 90,000–100,000-step checkpoints; the 4MIDD sign count was weaker after correction. These are differences in decoded output across model, phase, and seed. They do not show that internal implementations differ across seeds. The three official-cohort associations with Sintel endpoint error did not survive Holm correction, so task performance did not reliably account for the measured output variation. For signed A, the estimate was Spearman $\rho=0.284$ (95% CI [−0.003, 0.534]), and phase variability was associated at $\rho=−0.326$; with N=50, these nonsignificant corrected tests do not exclude moderate associations.

### Network–decoder readout and biological context

Cross-swaps and broad decoder-input replacements show that the decoded magnitude and direction readout depend on network–decoder pairing and that T4/T5 input replacement also changes physical-motion output. Because only 9/90 mismatched pairs decoded both physical directions correctly, the cross-swap result cannot isolate a MIDD-specific pairing effect from broader readout failure. Agrochao et al. linked fly behavior to T4/T5 imbalance [@AgrochaoEtAl2020IllusoryMotionFlies]; the present interventions provide a qualitative circuit-level point of contact, but replace broad decoder-input populations rather than reproducing that neuronal perturbation or measuring behavior. Saved T4c direction-selectivity labels showed a suggestive association with phase-averaged 3MIDD C=48 A (permutation $p=0.0176$; Holm-adjusted $p=0.0528$), but the corrected result was inconclusive and does not establish T4c causality (Table S5A). In the fly, T4/T5 signals are integrated by downstream wide-field circuits such as lobula-plate tangential cells, whereas FlyVis maps network activity to a dense flow field with an artificial decoder.

### Limitations

Decoded flow is a model readout, not a direct measurement of fly or human perception. We did not compare it with behavior or conduct a matched quantitative human-psychophysics experiment. Because we did not compare against an unconstrained recurrent network, these results do not establish that the responses are specific to connectome constraints. The local-to-global result is limited to an affine scalar prediction under a centered eight-field rendering and conditional held-out-luminance evaluation. Decoder-input replacements are model interventions, not biological knockouts, and their effects on physical-motion outputs limit claims of MIDD selectivity. The classical detector comparison is a partial computational analogy; it does not identify a causal nonlinear stage in FlyVis. Future work can test candidate computations while preserving ordinary motion readout and compare model outputs with behavior under matched stimulus conditions.

## Methods

### FlyVis model and model cohorts

We used connectome-constrained recurrent FlyVis networks and their learned optical-flow decoders [@LappalainenEtAl2024FlyVis]. The reproducibility environment uses FlyVis v1.2.0, commit `92b3845cc426dd309a1a0e1b3890156c42e14021`. Official pretrained data are obtained with `flyvis download-pretrained`; `FLYVIS_ROOT_DIR` locates upstream models, results, and rendering caches separately from this repository's stimuli, precomputed analysis products, and custom checkpoints.

The official `flow/0000/000–049` ensemble supplies Figs. 1–2, the primary C=48 phase analysis in Fig. 4, expanded static/task-performance controls, and native S1 calibration. Fig. 3, Fig. 6 decoder-input replacement, and Supplementary Fig. S9 use experiment-specific ten-model subsets (000–009). Fig. 5 uses learning-custom seeds 000–013. Recurrent subtype interventions use established seeds 001, 002, 003, 010 (002 holdout) and comparators 000, 008 at step 250,000. Cross-swap uses official models 000, 005, 010, 015, 020, 025, 030, 035, 040, 045. Matching numeric IDs do not imply matching models across sets. Cohorts and analysis-specific measurement conditions are summarized in Table S1.

### MIDD stimuli and rendering

For the single-BoxEye stimuli, `src/flyvis_midd/stimuli.py` constructs 3MIDD Original sectors by repeating `[0,C,255]` 24 times and Mirror sectors by repeating `[255,C,0]`. The balanced negative repeats `[0,C,0,255,C,255]` 12 times. Each pattern therefore contains 72 equal-angle sectors. The 4MIDD Original repeats `[0,C1,255,C2]` 24 times; Mirror repeats `[0,C2,255,C1]`; the negative repeats `[0,C1,255,C2,0,C2,255,C1]` 12 times, yielding 96 sectors. Mirror denotes the specified change in spatial luminance order, not contrast inversion. The negative arrangements balance directed transitions and are analyzed as distinct stimuli.

The renderer uses 382×382-pixel images, inner/outer annulus diameters of 55/382 pixels, phase 0°, and white pixels outside the stimulus annulus. Integer luminances are divided by 255 before model input. The fixed native reference conditions are 3MIDD C=48 and C=207 and 4MIDD C1=85/C2=170. Fig. 2 uses the 15 C levels `16,32,48,64,80,96,112,128,143,159,175,191,207,223,239` plus that fixed 4MIDD condition.  Static movies repeat one image for 50 frames.

Single-BoxEye movies use `BoxEye(extent=15, kernel_size=13)` (721 receptors; shape `(50,1,721)`). Fig. 3 uses a centered `(3,2,3)` eight-field layout (decoded shape `(8,50,2,721)`) and luminances `48,80,112,143,175,191,207`; S9 activity uses the same layout, while replacement analyses include BoxEye and `highres_232`, with results kept rendering-specific. For Fig. 3, the image is centered on a black 3:2 canvas with horizontal padding and row offsets [0,1,0]. The high-resolution helper fits and bilinearly resizes the canvas (`align_corners=False`, rounded dimensions); placement is right-aligned at the layout's upper edge. Receptor values average 13×13 patches with zero padding. Global aggregation rounds (y,x) coordinates to eight decimals and averages coincident vectors per frame, negates v once, and averages tangent projection (dy/r,−dx/r) over unique positions and frames 10–49 within the 0.14–0.98 image-radius annulus centered on the placed-image bounds. This multi-field basis is opposite to the single-BoxEye clockwise basis (−dy/r,dx/r); eight-field calibration yields positive CCW and negative CW scalars in all ten models and does not automatically transfer to the seven-field S2 layout.

### Stimulus presentation and decoded optical flow

Static movies use 50 Hz (`dt=0.02 s`), 50 frames, and 1-s fade-in/state preparation. Figs. 1–2 use `run_flow_inference`: CPU float32 luminance, network/decoder `.eval()`, `torch.no_grad()`, `network.fade_in_state(1.0,dt,luminance[:,0])`, then `network.simulate(luminance,dt,initial_state=initial_state)` and decoder flow. Fade-in frames are excluded from the recorded movie. `analyze_midd_flow` measures canonicalized output at frames 10–49; `aggregate_midd_metrics` computes A/S/M and raw reversal, with negative responses separate. Expanded official controls and phase runs use the same inference; composition, learning, interventions, and cross-swap use their specified preparations and saved outputs.

Decoder flow is canonicalized to `(sample,frame,2,receptor)` with components `(u,v)`; the zero-based analysis window is frames 10–49. Fig. 1 representatives are the reversal/non-reversal models nearest each group's median `(abs(T_O)+abs(T_M))/2`; vector snapshots use frame 25. This descriptive selection defines no weak-response class.

The S1 control uses static, clockwise, and counterclockwise three-blade propeller movies with 50 frames at 50 Hz and 128×128-pixel images. Direction agreement requires positive clockwise and negative counterclockwise `signed_tangent_ratio`. Rotation detection additionally requires the smaller moving-condition absolute ratio to exceed 0.1 and the smaller moving mean-flow magnitude divided by the static mean-flow magnitude to exceed 3.0. Direction agreement and detection are separate endpoints; propeller Static is not the MIDD negative condition.

### Tangential rotation and Original/Mirror decomposition

Main single-BoxEye analyses use `image_rotation_flow_metrics` in `src/flyvis_midd/rotation.py`. Receptor centers are in image coordinates, with x rightward and y downward. Decoder vectors are converted exactly once as `(u,v)→(u,−v)` before projection. For receptor displacement `(dx_i,dy_i)` from the mean receptor center and radius r_i, the clockwise tangent is `(-dy_i/r_i,dx_i/r_i)`. The analysis selects `0.14 r_max ≤ r_i ≤ 0.98 r_max`; the official single-BoxEye tables contain 698 selected receptors. `mean_signed_tangent`, denoted T, is the arithmetic mean of vector–tangent dot products over selected receptors and analyzed frames. Positive T denotes clockwise rotation. Values are decoded-flow units, not psychophysical velocities.

`signed_tangent_ratio` divides T by the mean Euclidean flow magnitude in the same window/annulus, using machine epsilon as a denominator floor. It is used for S1 detection and is not substituted for T in the main A/S analyses. Precomputed scalars already use the coordinate conversion defined for each experiment and are not flipped again. The multi-field global scalar and the activity/replacement analyses' image-radius ROI use their experiment-specific definitions rather than the single-BoxEye definition.

Within each model, condition, and checkpoint, we compute A=(T_O−T_M)/2, S=(T_O+T_M)/2, and M=|A|−|S|. Actual reversal uses the strict raw sign predicate T_O T_M<0, which is algebraically equivalent to M>0. Negative responses do not enter these definitions. Subtracting a common T_N preserves A but alters S and the raw reversal measure. Official-50 component distributions provide descriptive context in Supplementary Fig. S5; Fig. 4 combines the primary official-50 C=48 phase analysis with separate ten-model placement and radial analyses.

### Scalar composition and learning-dynamics analyses

For Fig. 3, A3(C) and observed A4 are Original/Mirror half-differences; the predictor is A3(C1)−A3(C2). Each model has an intercept OLS fit over 21 canonical C1<C2 pairs, with $R^2=1-\Sigma(y-\hat y)^2/\Sigma(y-\bar y)^2$. Identity residuals are observed A4 minus the unfitted predictor; RMSE is normalized by the A3 range. Metrics are averaged equally across models, not pooled across 210 pairs. Sign accuracy uses the unfitted predictor for evaluable pairs (18–21/model), defined by |A3(C1)−A3(C2)|/range(A3)≥0.05. This scalar analysis does not test full-vector additivity.

For Fig. 5, the checkpoint archive contains 2,586 files: 2,567 regular checkpoints, including initialization at step 0, and 19 irregular extras excluded from the trajectories. Regular support comprises step 0 and available 1,000-step intervals through each seed's endpoint. A/S/M are computed from paired Original/Mirror responses at each checkpoint. Operational onset is evaluated over fixed thresholds 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, and 0.005 using both first crossing and the first of three consecutive regular checkpoints above threshold. Gaps are not bridged. Noncrossers are censored, and crossing medians explicitly describe crossers. Primary summaries use common steps 0–100,000. We additionally compute AUC and time to a fixed fraction of final |A| as retrospective, threshold-free descriptors. Normalized magnitude is |A|/G, with G the mean Original/Mirror Euclidean flow magnitude in the same annulus/window; zero denominators are flagged. At each step, medians and interquartile ranges use only seeds with data at that step; checkpoint observations are repeated measurements within seeds.

Fixed-evaluation Sintel EPE uses 15 samples from sequence IDs `0,5,10,15,22`, no augmentation or random temporal cropping, 19 source frames resampled/interpolated to 40 evaluated frames at `dt=0.02 s`, and a center-crop fraction of 0.7. This diagnostic evaluation is not held out and is distinct from training loss. The same regular checkpoint support is required for MIDD and EPE tables. Official-50 evaluation uses MultiTaskSintel with three vertical splits of original sequence IDs 0,5,10,15,22 (15 samples), all_frames=False and resampling/interpolation enabled. Model/decoder evaluation mode and no_grad are used after a 0.5-s steady-state preparation. Batches of four (last batch three) are weighted by sample count; EPE is the mean vector endpoint error over all 40 resampled frames and 721 receptors. Checkpoint hashes were verified against source model identities and canonical Original/Mirror responses checked before accepting EPE. The three official-50 EPE associations use A(C48, phase 0), its absolute value, and SD(A across phases)/mean paired G; 10,000 whole-model bootstrap intervals and Holm correction across these relations are reported.

### Cell activity and interventions

The decoder-input activity analysis examines activity from 34 decoder-input cell types in ten models. Moving-edge calibration specifies 12 directions at 30° intervals, intensities 0 and 1, speed 19, `dt=0.005 s`, offsets `[-10,11]`, height 80, and 1 s pre/post periods. The primary preferred direction is leave-one-model-out (LOMO). MIDD cell evidence is the projected Original/Mirror half-difference, normalized and screened for identifiability as defined below. Signed and absolute Spearman coupling are evaluated within models across the seven C levels. Supplementary Fig. S9 shows these identifiable activity entries; available model counts vary by subtype.

Decoder-input replacement substitutes a frame-matched baseline for targeted channels without re-simulating the recurrent network. The corrected-family endpoint is normalized loss $L=1-$gain; T4a is primary, T4b exploratory, and T4/T5 groups are defined separately. No-op and nontarget-invariance checks assess specificity; alternate estimates are not pooled. For the decoder-input activity analysis, raw evidence e(C) is the space/time mean of the Original/Mirror activity half-difference multiplied by the LOMO preferred-direction tangent weight within the common annulus. Across seven C levels, R=sqrt(mean_C e(C)²), normalized evidence is e(C)/(R+10⁻⁸), and identifiability requires R>max_C|e_negative(C)|+10⁻⁸. For the decoder-input replacement analysis, the baseline averages intact activity over all 21 3MIDD conditions (seven luminances × Original/Mirror/negative), preserving frame, spatial sample, cell channel, and receptor position. Only targeted channels are replaced. Given intact and intervention seven-luminance A3 vectors x and y, gain=(y·x)/(x·x), without an intercept; loss=1−gain and shape residual=||y−gain x||/||x||. Gain is undefined if x·x is nonfinite or ≤10⁻¹². Bootstrap intervals are computed by resampling model losses with replacement 50,000 times, using random seed 10012 and percentile 2.5/97.5 intervals for the mean. The one-sided sign-flip test enumerates all 2ⁿ sign assignments and uses p=(1+number of permuted means≥observed mean)/(2ⁿ+1). T4a baseline is a singleton confirmatory family (q=p); TmY14, T2a, and Tm16 baseline form the secondary family; group interventions form a separate family; remaining single-type baseline interventions form the exploratory screen. Other interventions retain their catalog families. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment is applied separately within each nonsingleton family. Separately assigned targets are not included in the exploratory family's multiple-comparison correction.

Recurrent interventions neutralize T4c/T4d subtypes and compare matched-random node sets in the same instances (199 draws). MIDD summaries average twelve phases (0°–13.75°, 1.25° steps); paired Sintel outcomes are intervention-minus-intact EPE. The state hook zeros selected activity during MIDD fade-in/simulation and evaluated Sintel frames (after 0.5-s gray preparation). Each random set samples 721 T4 nodes without replacement within twelve sectors, matching target subtype counts; all T4 subtypes, including the target, are eligible. Seed/target/draw sets are reused across phases and Sintel samples. Sintel uses evaluation-role rows of `part4_sintel_scene_disjoint_split.csv` (indices 36,39,40,41,48,49,50; original sequences 12,13,16), excluding calibration sequences 2,7,9. MultiTaskSintel uses three vertical splits, 19 frames, 0.7 center crop, `dt=0.02 s`, resampling/interpolation, and no augmentation or random temporal crop. EPE differences are averaged across splits within sequence, then equally across sequences per seed; random controls retain draw identity. These are model perturbations, not biological knockouts.

Cross-swap is a descriptive 10×10 network–decoder design for 3MIDD C=48 (1-s gray preparation; 50 frames; stable window 10–49). Matched pairs share IDs; each network has nine mismatched decoders. A fixed balanced two-way centered sum-of-squares decomposition assigns row-mean and column-mean deviations to network and decoder, with residual $E_{ij}=Y_{ij}-\bar Y_{i\cdot}-\bar Y_{\cdot j}+\bar Y$; components sum to total centered SS. MIDD A and physical-grating $Q=(\bar u_{\mathrm{right}}-\bar u_{\mathrm{left}})/2$ are analyzed separately; early/late windows and |Q| are sensitivity endpoints. Ten thousand joint-identity bootstrap draws preserve matched cells. Intervals are conditional robustness summaries, not causal variance components; with one observation per pair, residual and measurement error cannot be separated. Within-column row and within-row column permutations are alignment diagnostics. Gratings use three spatial cycles, one temporal cycle/s, contrast 0.35, 50 frames at `dt=0.02 s` after 1-s gray preparation. Correct directions require mean rightward u>0 and leftward u<0 over the original grating receptors at frames 10–49. Both-direction accuracy differs from Q>0; original Q is checked first. Nine mismatches are summarized within network before matched/mismatched comparisons.

### Additional biological and circuit constraints

Saved T4c phenotype labels were compared with phase-averaged 3MIDD C=48 responses in the official 50-model set. Mean signed $A$ was −0.007707 for upward-selective (n=31), +0.002674 for downward-selective (n=7), and +0.002613 for non-direction-tuned (n=12) models. The cluster comparison was suggestive but inconclusive after correction (eta-squared=0.157, permutation p=.0176, Holm p=.0528); phase-instability and sign-change comparisons were also inconclusive (Holm p=.684 and .301; Table S5A). No independent broader biological-response score was available, so no composite fidelity measure was constructed. This association does not establish T4c causation or general prediction of MIDD output.

In the official 50-model cohort, the fixed equal-weight T4/T5 activity projection did not establish a common directional readout or model-level association with decoded $A$: the mean activity component was +0.000141 (95% CI [−0.000677, +0.001055], Holm p=1.000), its association with decoded $A$ was Spearman rho=.116 (95% CI [−.193, .415], Holm p=1.000), and sign agreement was 27/50 (54%; Table S5B). This is inconclusive for the tested fixed aggregate readout and is not evidence that T4/T5 activity contains no MIDD-related signal.

A separate broad ON-minus-OFF source-current summary was likewise inconclusive: mean $I_{ONOFF}$ was 4.52×10⁻⁸ (95% CI [−1.12×10⁻⁷, 2.16×10⁻⁷], Holm p=1.000), its association with decoded $A$ was Spearman rho=.065 (95% CI [−.216, .341], Holm p=1.000), and descriptive sign agreement was 26/50 (52%; Table S5B). A secondary OFF_A association with decoded $A$ was negative (Spearman rho=−.357, Holm-adjusted p=.048 within the secondary family), but remains a secondary observational result and does not identify a pathway mechanism. No D2 causal intervention was performed.
